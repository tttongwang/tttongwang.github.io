01ABOUT

Where statistical theory meets clinical practice.

Rare diseases collectively affect roughly one in ten Americans, yet approximately 95% of recognized rare conditions have no FDA-approved treatment. This unmet need motivates my interest in statistical methods that can help generate reliable clinical evidence when patient populations are small.

My Ph.D. research at Temple University investigated bias, statistical inference, and regret in adaptive data collection, leading to published work on the randomized multi-armed bandit (rMAB) framework.

Professionally, I work in pharmaceutical R&D on immunology programs, including clinical trial design, dose optimization, and evaluation of efficacy and safety. This experience provides practical insight into patients' needs, regulatory expectations, and the evidence required for sound treatment decisions.

These experiences help me pursue independent research that is both mathematically rigorous and clinically relevant. My methodological research is separate from my employer's drug development programs, while informed by the practical challenges I encounter in clinical research.

01aWHY IT MATTERS

THE PEOPLE BEHIND THE NUMBERS

Rare should never mean overlooked.

In the United States, a rare disease generally affects fewer than 200,000 people. Each condition may be uncommon, but together rare diseases touch millions of lives. For patients and families, the need for answers—and for effective treatments—is anything but small.

<200kPeople in the U.S. affected by a condition for it to be classified as rare
30m+Americans living with one of more than 10,000 rare diseases
~5%Of rare conditions have an FDA-approved treatment
01 / THE GAP

An unmet need, not a small need.

Many rare conditions are serious or life-threatening. Some patients face years without a diagnosis, while most rare diseases have no approved therapy. Scientific attention and the opportunity to benefit from research should not depend on how common a diagnosis is.

02 / THE CHALLENGE

Every participant matters.

When eligible patients are few and widely dispersed, conventional large clinical trials may be impractical. Limited knowledge of disease progression, heterogeneous symptoms, and scarce data make it harder to design studies and draw reliable conclusions.

03 / THE RESPONSE

Better methods can open doors.

This is why I care about rigorous, fit-for-purpose statistical research: adaptive designs, careful uncertainty assessment, transparent simulations, and open-source tools. These approaches can help us learn responsibly from limited evidence without compromising standards for safety or efficacy.

02RESEARCH

Research interests.

Methodology → Validation → Application
01 / FOUNDATION

Reliable inference in adaptive data collection

How can adaptive experiments learn efficiently while preserving trustworthy estimates and statistical error control? My doctoral work examined the theoretical and empirical behavior of randomized multi-armed bandits, including bias reduction, Type I error, and regret.

02 / APPLICATION

More feasible clinical trials for rare diseases

Rare-disease studies face small eligible populations, uneven recruitment, and difficult design trade-offs. I am exploring adaptive recruitment strategies and decision methods that may help researchers use limited trial resources more effectively while maintaining rigorous evaluation of safety and efficacy.
